\begin{abstract}
Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) and GraphSAGE represent foundational paradigms in geometric deep learning. While full-batch GCN operates across the entire graph topology, it encounters steep memory scaling bottlenecks on large graphs. GraphSAGE addresses this via stochastic neighborhood sampling and mini-batch optimization. In this report, we evaluate full-batch GCN on \texttt{ogbn-arxiv} and mini-batch GraphSAGE on \texttt{ogbn-products}, demonstrating empirically that full-batch GCN encounters memory exhaustion on million-scale graphs and providing theoretical justification for why stochastic sampling resolves this bottleneck. We examine model depth, learning rates, mini-batch sizes, and neighbor sampling budgets under controlled training budgets, providing empirical profiling of runtime efficiency and GPU memory allocation alongside formal scalability bounds.
\end{abstract}

\section{Task 1: Full-Batch GCN Reproduction \& Convergence}

\subsection{Experimental Configuration}
Full-batch GCN was evaluated on the \texttt{ogbn-arxiv} citation network ($|V| = 169,343$, $|E| = 1,166,243$, 40 subject classes). The baseline architecture employs a 3-layer GCN with hidden dimension $d = 256$, batch normalization, dropout $p = 0.5$, and ReLU activations, trained with Adam and a constant learning rate ($\eta = 0.01$). The baseline converged over 100 epochs, while all hyperparameter sensitivity runs were executed under a standardized 80-epoch budget.

\subsection{Convergence Dynamics}
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:gcn_conv}, GCN exhibits rapid loss reduction during the initial 40 epochs (training loss declining from $4.21$ to $1.05$). In the first 15 epochs, validation accuracy exhibits visible oscillation, likely reflecting batch normalization statistics stabilizing under full-batch gradient steps. A modest train--validation gap emerges in later epochs (evaluated training accuracy with dropout inactive reaches $73.59\%$ while validation accuracy plateaus at $71.82\%$), reflecting mild overfitting typical of citation networks without label-propagation regularization. Following these dynamics, validation accuracy stabilizes around epoch 70. The reference 100-epoch baseline run reaches a peak validation accuracy of \textbf{71.82\%} and test accuracy of \textbf{71.30\%}. Evaluating across 5 random seeds (seeds 42--46) to quantify run-to-run variation yields a mean validation accuracy of $71.92\% \pm 0.10\%$ and mean test accuracy of \textbf{71.16\% $\pm$ 0.26\%} (the reference run is a separate execution from the 5-seed set; standard GPU nondeterminism accounts for minor variance between individual seed-42 executions). This falls within $\approx 0.6$ pp of the official OGB test baseline ($71.74\% \pm 0.29\%$), demonstrating robust reproduction despite training for 100 epochs versus the reference 500 epochs.

\paragraph{Impact of Model Depth ($L$):} 
Increasing depth from $L=2$ to $L=3$ expands the receptive field to 3 hops, improving test accuracy by $1.22$ pp ($69.66\% \to 70.88\%$). This gain is $\approx 4.7\times$ the observed test standard deviation ($\sigma_{\text{test}} \approx 0.26$ pp), supporting the receptive-field expansion benefit. Selecting on validation accuracy, $L=4$ yields the highest value ($71.60\%$ val, $70.65\%$ test), marginally outperforming $L=3$ ($71.49\%$ val); the $0.11$ pp gap is comparable to the observed validation variation ($\sigma_{\text{val}} \approx 0.10$ pp) and consistent with run-to-run noise. In contrast, the drop from $L=4 \to L=6$ ($1.28$ pp val) exceeds the observed seed-to-seed std ($\approx 0.10$ pp) by an order of magnitude, suggesting genuine degradation rather than noise. We hypothesize that this performance drop stems from a combination of optimization challenges (such as gradient attenuation across deep stacks) and early oversmoothing. Specifically, the propagation rule (Equation~\ref{eq:gcn_layer}):
\begin{equation}
H^{(l+1)} = \sigma\left(\tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}} H^{(l)} W^{(l)}\right)
\label{eq:gcn_layer}
\end{equation}
repeatedly averages representations over expanding neighborhoods, progressively smoothing node feature variance toward stationary eigenvectors proportional to $\sqrt{\tilde{d}_i}$. While batch normalization stabilizes layer activations against internal covariate shift, it may not fully counteract Dirichlet energy decay across distant hops. Peak allocated memory (\texttt{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated}) grows linearly by $\approx 585$ MB per additional layer (from 5,445 MB at $L=2$ to 7,788 MB at $L=6$) due to caching intermediate activation maps for backpropagation.

\paragraph{Impact of Learning Rate ($\eta$):}
A small learning rate ($\eta = 0.001$) converges slowly within 80 epochs ($68.91\%$ val), while an aggressive rate ($\eta = 0.05$) exhibits gradient variance and plateaus at $69.33\%$ val. The val-optimal setting is $\eta = 0.005$ ($71.63\%$ val); however, the test gap versus $\eta = 0.01$ is only $0.01$ pp ($70.87\%$ vs.\ $70.88\%$)---well below the empirical test noise threshold ($\sigma \approx 0.26$ pp). Small runtime variations ($0.013$--$0.015$s) reflect host scheduling noise.

GraphSAGE was evaluated on the large-scale \texttt{ogbn-products} graph ($|V| = 2,449,029$, $|E| = 61,859,140$, 47 classes). The architecture utilizes 3 layers ($d = 256$), mean aggregation, dropout $p = 0.5$, and batch normalization.

\subsection{Convergence \& Replication}
GraphSAGE converged steadily over 15 epochs (Figure~\ref{fig:sage_conv}), achieving \textbf{91.61\%} validation accuracy and \textbf{78.61\%} test accuracy, within $0.1$ pp of the official OGB benchmark ($78.70\% \pm 0.36\%$). The notable validation-test gap ($91.61\%$ vs. $78.61\%$) is expected: OGB products uses a sales-ranking-based split where popular products comprise training and tail products form test sets, introducing an intentional distribution shift across splits.

\paragraph{Impact of Batch Size ($B$):}
Increasing batch size from 512 to 2048 decreases epoch runtime from $66.29$s to $49.94$s. With 196,615 training nodes, larger batches reduce total iteration steps per epoch ($\approx 385$ steps at $B=512$, $\approx 193$ at $B=1024$, and $\approx 97$ at $B=2048$), amortizing loop overhead and GPU kernel launch latency. Peak allocated memory remains nearly identical between $B=512$ and $B=1024$ ($\approx 7,110$ MB); because peak memory was tracked across the combined training and evaluation loop without a reset between phases, this flat memory plateau most likely reflects an eval-dominated peak memory confound ($B_{\text{eval}} = 4096$) that overshadows training differences at smaller batches. At $B=2048$, the larger training multi-hop union frontier finally exceeds the evaluation footprint, elevating peak memory to $9,393.7$ MB. By validation accuracy, $B=512$ marginally leads ($90.80\%$ vs.\ $90.77\%$); the difference is within single-seed noise and the two configurations are effectively indistinguishable.

\paragraph{Impact of Sampling Fanout ($S$):}
Comparing fanout configurations at 5 epochs reveals a clear trade-off: reducing fanout from $[15, 10, 5]$ to $[5, 5, 5]$ provides a \textbf{3.5$\times$ speedup} ($59.45$s $\to 16.92$s) and reduces peak allocated memory by $47\%$ ($7,109.7$ MB $\to 3,735.0$ MB), at the cost of a $1.97$ pp drop in test accuracy ($77.89\% \to 75.92\%$). Crucially, evaluating asymmetric layer-wise budgets with identical total path products ($15 \times 5 \times 5 = 5 \times 5 \times 15 = 375$ paths) isolates the role of hop placement: front-loading the budget at hop 1 ($S=[15, 5, 5]$) achieves $90.80\%$ val and $77.29\%$ test in $37.31$s, whereas back-loading at hop 3 ($S=[5, 5, 15]$) yields $89.27\%$ val and $75.52\%$ test ($1.77$ pp test deficit). One plausible hypothesis is that dense sampling at immediate neighbors ($S_1$) may better capture vital local graph topology, whereas heavy 3rd-hop sampling ($S_3$) aggregates increasingly diluted features across distant nodes; however, multi-seed replication is necessary to verify this effect. Empirically, a rough linear fit ($n=5$ configs) indicates approximately linear scaling with sampled path product according to $M \approx 2.9\text{ GB} + (\text{path product} \times 5.6\text{ MB})$, predicting $\approx 5.0\text{ GB}$ for the 375-path configurations (measured $\approx 5.5\text{ GB}$) and $\approx 5.7\text{ GB}$ for 500 paths (measured $6.15\text{ GB}$); this reflects sampled batch tensors atop a fixed baseline of static indexing and evaluation buffers.

\subsection{Hardware Scaling \& Slope-Based Extrapolation}
On \texttt{ogbn-arxiv}, Table~\ref{tab:gcn_ablation} demonstrates that full-batch GCN activation memory increases by $\approx 585$ MB per layer for 169K nodes. When scaling to \texttt{ogbn-products} ($2.45\text{M}$ nodes, a $14.5\times$ increase in $|V|$ at fixed hidden dimension $d = 256$), the activation memory slope scales proportionally with $|V|$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta M_{\text{layer}} \approx 585\text{ MB} \times 14.5 \approx 8.5\text{ GB per layer}
\end{equation}
For a 3-layer model, caching activations alone requires an estimated $\approx 25.5\text{ GB}$ (a lower bound: the slope excludes the unattributed baseline overhead of $\approx 4.3$ GB extrapolated to $L=0$ from Table~\ref{tab:gcn_ablation}, representing static graph tensors, Adam optimizer state tensors, and full-graph evaluation temporary buffers rather than CUDA driver runtime context, which \texttt{max\_memory\_allocated} does not track; note that if parts of this baseline scale with $|V|$, such as full-graph evaluation buffers, the $25.5$ GB estimate is a conservative lower bound, further reinforcing the OOM result). Adding the symmetrized \texttt{edge\_index} for 61.8M undirected edges ($123.7\text{M}$ directed pairs $\times 2$ rows $\times 8$ bytes $\approx 1.98\text{ GB}$) and intermediate aggregation buffers pushes the true requirement well beyond 30 GB. When executing full-batch GCN on \texttt{ogbn-products} on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (15.0 GB VRAM), PyTorch immediately halts with a \texttt{torch.cuda.OutOfMemoryError} (log in \texttt{results/oom\_log.txt} in the repository). In contrast, mini-batch GraphSAGE trains stably on the same GPU, consuming $7.1$ GB VRAM (and only $3.7$ GB under $[5,5,5]$).

\subsection{Theoretical Scalability Analysis: Resolving Neighborhood Explosion}
The memory divergence arises from the receptive field expansion during backpropagation:

\paragraph{Full-Batch Activation Bottleneck:}
Full-batch GCN computes layer activations globally: $H^{(l+1)} = \sigma(\tilde{A}_{\text{norm}} H^{(l)} W^{(l)})$. Exact gradient computation requires retaining all intermediate activation tensors $\{H^{(0)}, \dots, H^{(L)}\}$, imposing an $\mathcal{O}(L \cdot |V| \cdot d)$ memory footprint.

Furthermore, naive node-wise mini-batching without sampling fails due to exponential receptive field growth. In real-world graphs exhibiting power-law degree distributions (average degree $d_{\text{avg}} = 2|E|/|V| \approx 50.5$, or $\approx 25.3$ undirected edge pairs per node on \texttt{ogbn-products}), an unconstrained $L$-hop receptive field expands exponentially. For $L=3$, the branching paths from a single seed expand as $(50.5)^3 \approx 1.28 \times 10^5$; across a mini-batch of $B = 1024$ seed nodes, the union of overlapping receptive fields can cover a large fraction of the active graph.

\paragraph{Stochastic Neighborhood Sampling:}
GraphSAGE restricts neighbor aggregation to a fixed sample budget $S_l$ at each hop $l \in \{1, \dots, L\}$. For a mini-batch of $B$ seed nodes, the outermost layer frontier is strictly bounded by:
\begin{equation}
|\mathcal{V}_L| \le B \cdot \prod_{l=1}^L S_l
\label{eq:outer_frontier}
\end{equation}
while the total number of sampled nodes across all layers is bounded by $B \left(1 + \sum_{l=1}^L \prod_{i=1}^l S_i\right) = B(1 + S_1 + S_1 S_2 + \dots)$. This bounds per-step backpropagation activation memory to $\mathcal{O}\left(B \cdot d \cdot \sum_{l=1}^L \prod_{i=1}^l S_i\right)$, which is asymptotically dominated by the outermost frontier $\mathcal{O}(B \cdot d \cdot \prod_{l=1}^L S_l)$. While static node features and sampler indexing occupy a fixed GPU memory baseline ($\approx 980\text{ MB}$ for features in our setup, or CPU host memory in out-of-core pipelines), GPU activation memory during backpropagation is decoupled from total graph size $|V|$, enabling scalable representation learning on graphs of arbitrary magnitude.

\section{Reproducibility \& Environment Details}
Experiments were executed on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (15.0 GB VRAM, 2 vCPUs) with Python 3.11, PyTorch 2.11, PyG 2.4.0, and OGB 1.3.6 under fixed seed 42 across all single-run baselines and ablations, supplemented by seeds 42--46 for the GCN multi-seed evaluation in Section 1.2. GCN step times use \texttt{torch.cuda.synchronize()} on FP32 operations; GraphSAGE epoch times reflect sampling and training (excluding periodic evaluation passes). Multi-process loaders on 2 vCPUs introduce sampling bottlenecks due to CPU oversubscription. Complete code, reproduction scripts, and OOM logs are maintained in the GitHub repository (\url{https://github.com/vaishvi-code/CSC591-038-_hw2}).

\section{Conclusion}
This study reproduced GCN and GraphSAGE on OGB benchmarks. On validation accuracy, $L=4$ and $\eta=0.005$ led the GCN ablation grid, while $S=[15,5,5]$ achieved the highest validation accuracy ($90.80\%$) and faster runtime ($37.3$s) for GraphSAGE, with $S=[15,10,5]$ offering highest test accuracy ($77.89\%$). For GCN, validation differences between mid-range configurations ($0.11$ pp for $L \in \{3,4\}$; $0.14$ pp for $\eta \in \{0.005, 0.01\}$) are modest ($< 0.2$ pp) and close to the baseline validation run-to-run variation ($\sigma_{\text{val}} \approx 0.10$ pp across 5 seeds). For GraphSAGE, validation accuracy between $B=512$ ($90.80\%$) and $B=1024$ ($90.77\%$) differs by only $0.03$ pp, though multi-seed replication would be needed to establish formal variance bounds on products. Profiling confirms that stochastic neighborhood sampling bounds the backpropagation frontier and resolves receptive field explosion, enabling scalable mini-batch training where full-batch GCN encounters out-of-memory limits.
